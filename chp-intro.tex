\epigraph{The major challenge for language documentation in the next decade or two is what could be called the transcription challenge. This is a multilayered challenge that goes far beyond the practical challenge of speeding up the transcription process. [...] Despite its centrality to language documentation, transcription remains critically undertheorized and understudied. Further progress in language documentation, and ultimately also its overall success, crucially depends on further investigating and understanding the transcription process, broadly conceived.}%
         {\justifying{}Himmelmann, N. P. (2018). Meeting the transcription challenge. In B. McDonnell, A. Berez-Kroeker \& G. Holton (Eds.), \emph{Reflections on language documentation: 20 years after Himmelmann 1998}.}

Language documentation is typically considered a sub-field of linguistics and its goal is ``to provide a comprehensive record of the linguistic practices characteristic of a given speech community'' \parencite[][p.~166]{himmelmann1998documentary}.~Many aspects of its practice, however, require continual integration of developments beyond linguistics.~As the target languages are often spoken by minoritised Indigenous communities, it is necessary to proactively consider evolving norms regarding fair and ethical collaboration \parencite{holton2022indigenous}.~Concurrently, data protocols must not only continually integrate these norms but also technological advancements that more effectively enable the creation, processing, storage, and distribution of recorded materials \parencite{berez2023recent}.~In both respective areas, there are ongoing developments that constitute paradigm shifts:~1) decolonising, community-centred approaches to working with Indigenous communities, and 2) speech and language processing systems powered by artificial intelligence (AI), specifically large-scale pre-trained models.~Integrating considerations from the former and leveraging advancements in the latter, I examine in this dissertation how large-scale pre-trained models can be leveraged to develop speech processing systems to create contextually-appropriate solutions to combat a major bottleneck in language documentation workflows --- 
the transcription of recorded materials.

\section{Language documentation and the transcription bottleneck}

\corr{The goal of language documentation is to produce a multi-purpose record of a language \parencite{himmelmann1998documentary}.~Given this goal, the recorded materials may encompass a variety of linguistic topics and cultural practices and, consequently, materials appropriate for one purpose (e.g.~linguistic analyses) may be entirely inappropriate for others (e.g.~creating language learning materials).~Thus, the ability to efficiently locate and extract only the relevant information from recorded materials is a need shared among in a wide variety of use cases --- from linguistic and lexicographical analyses, to development of language materials, to simply wanting to listen to the speech of one's elders.~In this section, we examine how transcription plays a central role in meeting the various information needs for many diverse uses, how needs often go unmet due to difficulties of creating transcriptions (§\ref{sec:transcription-bottleneck}), and two pathways for better meeting these needs (§\ref{sec:alleviating}).}

\subsection{Information needs met via transcription of recorded materials}
\label{sec:info-needs}

It is generally recognised that there are about 7,000 languages spoken in the world today and that at least half of them may not exist by the end of the century \parencite{austin2011cambridge}.~Many of these languages are spoken by Indigenous and minoritised communities, who are under cultural, economic, and technological pressures to shift to using more dominant regional, national, or global languages.~For example, education may be conducted entirely in more widely-used regional or national languages and technological interfaces may only exist in a national or global language.~Such pressures can lead to language endangerment, as younger generations gradually adopt the more widely-used languages until there remain no living first-language or `L1' speakers, at which point a language may become `dormant'.~Thus, there are many ongoing revitalisation efforts to fight these pressures by encouraging the learning and active use of endangered languages, to safeguard the knowledge of elder L1 speakers by recording them, as well as efforts to ‘awaken’ dormant ones based on archival recordings.

\corr{Given a collection of recordings, the conventional workflow for making the collection searchable for various uses is to create time-aligned text annotations for each recording.~As illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:annotation} using a Samoan utterance from my Field Methods class recordings, these annotations typically consist of a transcription of the utterance in either the target language orthography (if one exists) or in the International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA), a morpheme-by-morpheme gloss, and an utterance-level free translation of the utterance in a more widely-used vehicular language (e.g.~English).~These text annotations can be searched using plain text or regular expression queries.~For a linguistic analysis, one may be interested in all forms with a glottal onset in the final syllable, which can be retrieved based on a regular expression search on the transcriptions (e.g.~using the query `{\fontspec{LinLibertine.ttf}*ʔ[aeiou]+\$}').~For creating language learning materials illustrating verb tenses, one may want to find all instances of the verb [{\fontspec{LinLibertine.ttf}siʔi}] `to lift', which can be retrieved by either a search for `{\fontspec{LinLibertine.ttf}siʔi}' in the transcriptions or `lift' in the glosses and/or translations.~In both cases, a result of interest will be the instance occurring around the 30-second mark in the recording `20200218\_TF\_NS.wav' illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:annotation}.~In this way, these annotations play a central role in meeting a variety of information needs for many diverse uses.}

\corr{We note here two important details regarding information needs.~First, we examine various information needs through various downstream `uses' rather than `users'.~This is in recognition that linguists working in documentation are often required to `wear many hats' (working on theoretical analyses at times and creating language learning materials at others), and that historical divides such as [non-native] linguist vs.~[native speaker] community member are becoming increasingly tenuous given many instances of speaker-linguists working on their own languages.~Second, we note that certain uses require considerably more detailed annotations than others.~For example, morpheme-by-morpheme glosses would be essential for lexicographical analyses and the creation of a dictionary.~By contrast, even partial translations may suffice for broadly identifying regions in the recordings where various topics of interest are being discussed.~We return to this second fact below in examining how --- for certain uses --- some form of search and retrieval can be facilitated without the need for certain annotations that are time-consuming to create.}

\subsection{The transcription bottleneck: consequences and causes}
\label{sec:transcription-bottleneck}

\corr{As illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:doc-workflow}, the conventional workflow in language documentation projects consists of three broad stages:~1) collection,~2) annotation,~and 3) use.~As mentioned, the annotation stage (of which transcription is part) plays a central role in enabling audio recorded in the first stage to be searched and retrieved for various uses in later stages (e.g.~for creating linguistic analyses or language learning materials).}~However, as manually transcribing recorded speech is much more time-consuming than recording speech, a bottleneck is formed at the transcription stage.~As a consequence, many recordings within language documentation projects remain only partially transcribed, if at all \parencite{cox2019taking}.~Yet, raw audio data comprising untranscribed speech is difficult to index and search, limiting how efficiently content within the materials can be discovered and used.~Indeed, the consequences of hard-to-access materials are so dire that there are warnings against inadvertently creating ``data graveyards, i.e. large heaps of data with little or no use to anyone'' \parencite[][p. 4]{himmelmann2006language}.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{figures/intro-doc-workflow}

\label{fig:doc-workflow}
% \vspace{-1em}
\end{figure}

There are several contributing factors that result in this bottleneck with respect to transcribing minoritised languages.~First, unlike major languages such as English, searchable high-quality transcriptions cannot simply be derived using a high-performance automatic speech recognition (ASR) system, whose development itself has conventionally required large quantities of transcribed speech.~Second, there is typically no option for large-scale crowd-sourcing of target language transcriptions as by definition of endangerment there may be very few speakers and only a subset of speakers may be literate in this language (i.e.~not the regional or national language of formal education).~Third, there may not be a standardised orthography for the target language, requiring transcription in IPA or development of a project-specific working orthography, with both options resulting in transcriptions with both intra- and inter-transcriber variation, requiring additional time for review and corrections.~Finally, in some instances, there may also be an additional personnel bottleneck resulting from limitations on who can listen to and transcribe certain recordings (e.g.~of culturally-sensitive materials such as descriptions of ceremonial procedures).~Taken altogether, surveys of transcription time have reported typically requiring about 30 to 50 hours of work to transcribe one hour of recorded speech \parencite{durantin2017survey,michaud2014towards,zahrer2020towards}.

Reviewing a century of transcription practice within linguistics, \textcite{bird-2020-sparse} argues that some of these contributing factors result from a description-centred transcription practice, which we detail below.~For context, we note here that language documentation and description are considered two inter-dependent but different activities varying in outputs and goals \parencite{himmelmann1998documentary}.~Language documentation, which includes data collection and annotation, aims to produce a multi-purpose record of a language.~Its outputs are primary data that may be useful to a broad audience, including community members, language teachers, anthropologists, and those from other disciplines.~The outputs of language description are typically grammatical descriptions and theoretical analyses based on these descriptions that are more narrowly aimed at comparative linguists.~Historically, documentation processes have privileged the latter, which can result in primary data that is sub-optimal or inapplicable for other uses.~For example, description-focused data collection may lead to the collection of idealised forms of infrequent phenomena-of-interest rather than day-to-day colloquial or child-directed speech \parencite{rouvier2017language}, the latter two of which are more relevant to revitalisation efforts.~Spurred on in part by broader decolonial efforts by Indigenous communities, community-based and collaborative documentation methods that also contribute to community needs are emerging as best practices in linguistic research \parencite{holton2022indigenous}.

\textcite{bird-2020-sparse} argues that several parts of the transcription bottleneck result from a description-centred transcription practice.~He identifies various inefficient characteristics particular to orthodox transcription practice, two of which are `transcribing fully' and `transcribing first' (i.e.~translating second).~We review these characteristics using the aforementioned Samoan utterance from my Field Methods class recordings, repeated below for convenience as Figure \ref{fig:how-lift-boat-tf}.~Given my goal of grammatically analysing a language largely unknown to me, I am primarily interested in textually representing the form of the spoken utterance, leading to a primary focus on the phonetic form of the whole utterance [{\fontspec{LinLibertine.ttf}sasiʔielevailevaʔa}], with the translation secondary.~Using these annotations along with other data points, I can hypothesise what the various lexical and grammatical units are in Samoan and how they interact,~e.g.~[{\fontspec{LinLibertine.ttf}sa}]+[{\fontspec{LinLibertine.ttf}siʔi}] P{\footnotesize{}ST}+lift `lifted'.~\textcite{bird-2020-sparse} questions whether this description- and analysis-centred conceptualisation of transcription effectively serves the many parties interested in working with untranscribed language documentation corpora, particularly those of unwritten, oral languages.

\subsection{Two pathways for alleviating the transcription bottleneck}
\label{sec:alleviating}

\corr{As noted above, not all uses require fully transcribed, glossed, and translated recordings for discovering excerpts of interest within documentation corpora.~For example, a community member may simply wish to listen to excerpts of a certain elder or all excerpts relating to a broad topic (e.g.~place names).~Often, recording-level metadata and/or accompanying field notes or wordlists can help offer a rough index of the recordings, though such data sources may not always be available --- especially in the case of archival recordings.~Indeed, as originally pointed out by \textcite{bird-2020-sparse}, there is little reason for search and retrieval for these uses to await for the corpus to be fully transcribed or even the development of a target language orthography.~Consequently, one important research question (RQ1) for improving access is how to derive a rough but workable index of the recordings that bypasses target language transcription --- avoiding the transcription bottleneck altogether.}

\corr{Nevertheless, certain uses do require excerpts of interest to be fully transcribed, glossed, and translated.~For example, creating a dictionary of the language typically requires some linguistic and lexicographic analyses to catalogue the lexical and grammatical units in the excerpts,~e.g.~[{\fontspec{LinLibertine.ttf}sa}]+[{\fontspec{LinLibertine.ttf}siʔi}] P{\footnotesize{}ST}+lift `lifted'.~For uses that depend on prior analyses, the process of transcription remains an unavoidable part of the workflow.~One natural solution is of course to use automatic speech recognition (ASR) to derive first-pass transcriptions that can be corrected by a human, thereby reducing transcription time \parencite{sperber-etal-2016-optimizing,Sperber2017b,prud2021automatic}.~As mentioned, however, developing an ASR system has conventionally required a large quantity of transcribed speech which is hard to obtain --- leaving many minoritised languages in a Catch-22.~Consequently, a second important research question (RQ2) is how ASR systems can be developed using only limited amounts of transcribed speech by leveraging supplemental resources that are relatively easier to obtain (e.g.~text from books or websites,~untranscribed speech from news broadcasts).}

\corr{At a broader level, we may view solutions that address each of these two research questions (RQ1, RQ2) as use-informed pathways that, respectively, bypass or widen the transcription bottleneck, as illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:doc-workflow-alleviate}.~For uses that do not require detailed analyses of the spoken content, the first pathway helps provide a rough but workable index with approximate locations of key words and phrases of interest in a more widely spoken vehicular language (e.g.~English).~For uses that do require detailed analyses and hence transcriptions of the spoken content, the second pathway helps accelerate transcription efforts by leveraging an ASR system to create rough first-pass transcriptions that can be corrected by a human transcriber.~In this way, these pathways help diversify the avenues through which diverse information needs can be met.}

\label{fig:doc-workflow-alleviate}

\section{Alleviating the transcription bottleneck using pre-trained models}
\label{sec:outline}

\corr{In the previous section, I substantiated the importance of addressing two research questions related to alleviating the transcription bottleneck in language documentation projects.~These questions were:~RQ1) how to bypass the need for target language transcription and derive a searchable index in a more widely used vehicular language, and RQ2) how to make effective use of limited, hard-to-obtain target language transcriptions for ASR development by leveraging relatively easier-to-obtain supplemental resources.~As mentioned, solutions that address each of these research questions can be broadly viewed as diversifying the pathways that can help meet diverse information needs.~Given a diversity of scenarios in the kinds and amounts of available data from one documentation project to another, I propose for each pathway two possible implementations that each address a common but distinct scenario.

In this section, I provide high-level summaries of my implementations and key findings.~For these high-level summaries, I will refer to recently developed artificial intelligence (AI) models used in the various implementations collectively as `pre-trained models', a topic which will be discussed in detail in Chapter 2.~Then, in Chapters 3--6, I detail each implementation addressing a specific scenario and one of the two research questions, as summarised in Table \ref{tab:chapters}.~In Chapters 3--4, I propose two implementations for deriving a rough but workable index in a vehicular language in scenarios where no target transcriptions are obtainable (e.g.~due to a lack of a target language orthography).~In Chapters 5--6, I propose two implementations for ASR-assisted transcription in scenarios where a small amount of target language transcriptions are available.~In particular, I examine how to improve ASR system performance for delivering better quality first-pass transcriptions by drawing on supplemental resources that are relatively easier to obtain than transcribed speech (e.g.~texts from books or untranscribed speech).}

\subsection{Deriving a rough index in a vehicular language using pre-trained models}

\corr{As discussed in the previous section (§\ref{sec:transcription-bottleneck}), \textcite{bird-2020-sparse} identifies two attributes in the conventional annotation workflow that contribute heavily to the transcription bottleneck: transcribing fully and transcribing first (i.e.~translating second).~While these attributes are well-motivated for certain uses (e.g.~linguistic description), not all uses require such in-depth and thus time-consuming analyses of the spoken content.~To facilitate search and retrieval for these latter uses, \textcite{bird-2020-sparse} suggests relaxing these two attributes by, respectively: annotating sparsely (instead of fully) and using translations (instead of target language transcriptions).}

~As illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:sparse-bird}, \textcite{bird-2020-sparse} proposes a supplementary annotation workflow where spoken term detection or `word spotting' is used to facilitate searches via spoken queries (i.e.~a voice-based alternative to text queries on transcriptions) and, upon confirmation of hits, time-aligned translations can be provided in a vehicular language (i.e.~one with a standardised orthography in which speakers are literate).~The confirmed locations of various queries along with their translations allow for the corpus to be collaboratively and iteratively indexed, albeit sparsely (\corr{i.e.~at the outset, just key words of interest such as common verbs and nouns, as illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:sparse-bird}}).~Additionally, this approach can help more effectively find regions of interest to which more time-consuming conventional transcription efforts can be strategically directed (i.e.~it is intended as a supplementary not alternative approach to `full' transcription).

Building on this approach, I suggest an implementation for deriving a rough index in a vehicular language without spoken term detection or manual crowd-sourced translations for a specific type of speech corpus commonly found in documentation projects.~In language documentation, recordings may contain not only the target language being documented but also vehicular language used for questions and commentary about the target language, i.e.~the vehicular language (e.g.~English) is used as a metalanguage.~For example, as illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:mixed}, the target language response by the Samoan speaker around the 30-second mark was preceded by my elicitation prompt in English:~\textit{How do you say ``the water lifted the boat''?}~As mentioned above, it is relatively straightforward to derive searchable high-quality transcriptions using a high-performance ASR system for major languages such as English.~As such, I show that for this type of mixed-language corpora pre-trained models can be used to automatically isolate and transcribe the metalanguage.~These searchable metalanguage transcriptions in turn provide approximate locations where certain target language words and topics are being discussed.

\subsubsection{Chapter 3: Improving spoken term detection using pre-trained models}

As introduced above, spoken term detection could potentially make an untranscribed speech corpus more accessible by allowing users to perform voice searches and in turn permit confirmed hits to act a sparse index of the corpus.~Recent work investigating this potential discovered that while retrieval performance was tolerable in cases where the speakers of the search query and the searched recordings were the same, much worse performance was observed when the two were different \parencite{le-ferrand-etal-2020-enabling}.~Based on their comparison of various speech representations, \textcite{le-ferrand-etal-2020-enabling} suggested future work investigate whether recently developed pre-trained models offer more speaker-independent representations.

Accordingly, I compared the impact on retrieval performance as a result of using speech representations extracted from various pre-trained models as well as those from two baseline methods, using data from 7 Australian Aboriginal languages and Gronings, a variant of Low Saxon (one of the minority languages spoken in the Netherlands).

Results from these experiments showed that in the worst-case scenario retrieval performance is still improved 56--86\% from only 27--28\% of queries being retrievable for the best baseline to 42--52\% when using pre-trained models.~Given the alternative is browsing the untranscribed audio in near real-time, this level of performance helps enable \emph{some} form of search and retrieval to untranscribed speech corpora.

\subsubsection{Chapter 4: Deriving a metalanguage proxy index using pre-trained models}

As introduced in the previous section (§\ref{sec:transcription-bottleneck}), there may be access restrictions to recordings in a documentation project as a result of potential culturally-sensitive materials within these recordings.~However, for archival materials with insufficient metadata, it is hard to determine which parts of which recordings must be restricted without the custodian(s) manually listening to and vetting each recording in the collection.

I propose a privacy-preserving workflow to accelerate this vetting process for recordings where speech in the target language is intermixed with a more widely-used language such as English for meta-linguistic commentary and questions (e.g. \textit{What is the word for `tree'?}).~I propose integrating voice activity detection (VAD), spoken language identification (SLI), and automatic speech recognition (ASR) to isolate and transcribe the metalinguistic content, which an authorised person can quickly scan to triage recordings that can be annotated by people with lower levels of access.

I report results from an ongoing documentation project to process 136 hours archival audio containing a mix of English and Muruwari.~In this collaborative work with the Muruwari custodian of the archival materials and the documentation team, I show that this workflow reduces metalanguage transcription time by 20\% even with minimal amounts of annotated training data to adapt pre-existing task-specific models: as little as 10 utterances per language for SLI and 39 seconds for English ASR.~These metalanguage transcriptions not only allow the custodian to more efficiently vet and clear recordings (or hold certain ones back for further manual review), but also allow the documentation team to search the cleared materials for creating language revitalisation materials and linguistic analyses.

\subsection{ASR development with limited target language transcriptions leveraging pre-trained models and supplemental resources}

\corr{As discussed in the previous section (§\ref{sec:alleviating}), there are many uses which require in-depth analysis of the spoken language content and hence transcription of this content.~However, manual transcription of recordings in documentation projects can require between 30 to 50 hours of work to transcribe one hour of recorded speech \parencite{durantin2017survey,michaud2014towards,zahrer2020towards}.~While there has been a long-standing desire to accelerate the transcription process using automatic speech recognition (ASR), such systems have typically been out of reach for many documentation projects --- given large quantities of transcribed speech have typically been required to begin ASR system development.

Recently developed pre-trained models, however, have substantially reduced this upfront cost, demonstrating that ASR system development may now be possible with even an hour of transcribed speech or, in certain circumstances, as little as 10 minutes.~Naturally, this has spurred on much experimentation for leveraging such models for ASR-assisted transcription in language documentation workflows \parencite[e.g.][]{coto-solano-etal-2022-development,guillaume2022fine,macaire-etal-2022-automatic,bartelds-etal-2023-making}.~Understandably, given that transcribed speech is \emph{the} critical resource for ASR development, many of these investigations have focused on understanding the impacts on ASR system performance when using pre-trained models with various amounts of transcribed speech.~Complementing these investigations, I examined how to make most effective use of a fixed amount of transcribed speech by drawing on other easier-to-obtain resources.

In the broader ASR literature, `low resource' languages are typically classified as such primarily based on the availability of transcribed speech.~Other resources and information for these languages are assumed to be readily obtainable.~For example, it is assumed that text data is readily available from the web (e.g.~Wikipedia), untranscribed speech from radio or news broadcasts, or reliable metadata (e.g.~geographic location, genetic family) is available in typological databases.~Such assumptions, however, may not hold true for under-described languages with little digital representation typical in language documentation projects.~Accordingly, in my investigations presented in Chapters 5--6, I investigated when certain proposals within the low-resource ASR literature were directly applicable for use in documentation projects and possible alternative pathways for when they were not.}

\subsubsection{Chapter 5: Kick-starting ASR development using pre-trained models and supplemental texts}

As mentioned, recent experiments have demonstrated that ASR development may be possible with as little as 10 minutes of transcribed speech when leveraging pre-trained models and some supplemental text data.~The text data can be leveraged to create a lexicon that improves the performance of an ASR system by limiting its output to possible words (e.g.~*\textit{dogz} vs.~\textit{dogs}) and biasing the system towards probable word sequences (e.g.~\textit{too dogs} vs.~\textit{two dogs}).~These experiments, however, leveraged a pre-trained model developed using 960 hours of English audio and, crucially, an 803 million word corpus of English text.~Yet, language-specific pre-trained models and this much text data are unlikely to be available for minoritised languages.

I examined the real-world viability of this setup beyond English using a multilingual pre-trained model and conducted ASR experiments using 10 minutes of transcribed speech from two pairs of languages.~These languages differed in the availability of supplemental text data:~Gronings and Frisian (two West-Germanic languages for which \textasciitilde{}7.5M word corpora available) and Besemah and Nasal (two Malayo-Polynesian languages for which only small \textasciitilde{}10k word lists are available).

Results showed that this setup does appear promising for the two West-Germanic languages for which sufficient text data can be sourced and are over-represented in the training data on which the multilingual pre-trained model was developed  --- yielding an average word-error rate of 39\% with only 10 minutes of transcribed speech and as little as an 80k word corpus of text (\corr{i.e.~roughly the length of a novel}).~However, for the two under-represented, predominantly oral languages for which little text data is available, the ASR performance remained poor (65\% average) --- suggesting the need for a different setup for such languages.

\subsubsection{Chapter 6: Leveraging untranscribed speech to adapt pre-trained models for under-represented languages}

As European languages dominate the training data for multilingual pre-trained models, directly using the off-the-shelf pre-trained model for ASR for under-represented languages results in relatively lower performance compared to their well-represented counterparts \parencite{conneau2023fleurs}.~While recent studies have shown that these models can be adapted using untranscribed speech from the target language in turn closing this performance gap \parencite[e.g.][]{NOWAKOWSKI2023103148,paraskevopoulos2024sample}, they involved using 70–200 hours of target language data.~For some languages, it may be quite difficult to source this much speech data --- even untranscribed.

\newpage
For these languages, I propose carrying out the model adaptation process by supplementing limited amounts of target language data with data from a similar, higher-resource language (termed the `donor' language).~Additionally, I suggest that the acoustic-phonetic and phonotactic similarities between the target and donor languages predict improved ASR performance in the target language and propose a bottom-up measure to determine this similarity --- the Acoustic Token Distribution Similarity (ATDS).

Results showed improved ASR performance in the target language (e.g.~Punjabi) when models were adapted using supplemental data from a similar donor (e.g.~10h Punjabi + 60h Hindi) but not when using data from a dissimilar one (e.g.~10h Punjabi + 60h Bengali).~Across a set of typologically different target languages (Punjabi, Galician, Iban, Setswana), I show that the ATDS between the target language and its candidate donors precisely predicts target language ASR performance and outperforms all other similarity measures examined.
